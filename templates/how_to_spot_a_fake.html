The short version

A real venture fund leaves a paper trail. Fakes don't. Check it in a minute.

The social tells: they're easy to like

Fakes don't look fake. They look like the most connected person in the room. A room vouching for someone isn't a check. Paperwork is.

Well connected and well respected means nothing

Bernie Madoff's victims were not naive. They included Steven Spielberg's Wunderkinder Foundation, Elie Wiesel's foundation, Kevin Bacon and Kyra Sedgwick, Sandy Koufax, Larry King, Mets owner Fred Wilpon's family, John Malkovich, the charitable trust of New York Daily News owner Mortimer Zuckerman, major banks (Santander, HSBC, Royal Bank of Scotland, BNP Paribas), insurers (AXA, Sumitomo Life, Korea Life), universities (Yeshiva, NYU, Tufts, Bard) and charities. His own community, who trusted him most, was hit hard: Hadassah alone lost about $90 million. He was also registered with the SEC. Reputation, exclusivity and a filing did not protect any of them.

Sources: AP on the client list filed in bankruptcy court; Jewish Journal (Wunderkinder, Wiesel); Fortune (Bacon and Sedgwick); CNN Money (Wilpon, trustee’s suit); Biography (Malkovich); Philadelphia Inquirer (Zuckerman); CNBC (banks); investor list with WSJ and Reuters citations (insurers); Brown Daily Herald (universities); eJewishPhilanthropy (Hadassah); SEC Inspector General report (registration).

This applies to men and women alike. Look for the pattern, not the type:

  • Everywhere visible: parties, events, the VIP section, always in the right photos.
  • Exclusivity as the pitch: ‘by invitation,’ ‘closing soon,’ ‘I can get you in.’
  • Big claims, no names: 20 LPs, a huge fund, great returns, but no named co-investors, no portfolio companies you can verify.
  • Lifestyle as proof: the fancy car, the watches, the table. (Often rented or borrowed. They'll never say.)
  • Steady, too-good returns, or vague answers about how the money is made.
  • Name-dropping: past roles and famous friends that don't match public records.
  • Won't show documents: no offering memorandum, no fund agreement, no named lawyer or administrator.
  • Pressure to move fast, or to wire money to a personal account.
  • Asks you to bring friends.
  • Gets irritated or evasive when asked to be checked.

Charm is free. Filings aren't. Check the paperwork below.

1. If they're raising money from anyone else, there's a Form D

A fund that takes money from outside investors (LPs) almost always files a Form D with the SEC within 15 days of its first sale. Unless a VC is investing only their own money, there should be one. A single outside LP is enough to need it. The Form D shows who the fund is, who signed, the people running it, the date of first sale, the amount raised and how many investors.

No Form D is a red flag.

There is a way to raise money without filing a Form D (relying on the Section 4(a)(2) private placement exemption directly instead of the standard Reg D rules). It is risky for the fund and almost never done. And even then, a real fund usually shows up in other filings FURP finds anyway: Form ADV, SEC filings, 990s, registrations, and the people who run it.

No Form D is a red flag. No trace anywhere is the real tell.

2. If they advise funds, check Form ADV

Fund managers and advisers file Form ADV, either registered or as an exempt reporting adviser. Look them up.

3. If they're “raising money for other VCs,” they need a broker-dealer license

Anyone paid to find investors for a fund is generally acting as a broker-dealer and must be licensed. FURP checks broker-dealer records too.

4. What to look for on the result

  • Does the name match the one on the pitch, the website and the LinkedIn?
  • Do the people listed match who you're dealing with?
  • Is the amount raised and the date consistent with their story?
  • Is the fund new, with a “track record” that predates it?

5. Then check everything else

FURP also shows the nonprofits, filings and people connected to a name. A real investor shows up in several places.

Paperwork is a start, not proof

Having a filing doesn't mean someone isn't a fake. Not having one isn't definitive either. Use extreme caution either way. But checking the filings is the best first step to protect yourself: it takes a minute, costs nothing, and most fakes fail it.
