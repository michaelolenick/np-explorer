How to spot a fake

FAKE venture funds are a real and recurring problem. The SEC keeps bringing cases against people who ran purported venture funds or dressed up a ‘VC firm’ with fake documents.*** People call themselves VCs, take the meetings, ask for fees, introductions or money, and have no fund behind them, or a negligible one. This page shows how to spot one, and checks a fund, firm or person against the filings in a minute.

The short version

A real venture fund leaves a paper trail. Fakes don't, or leave a very recent or very thin one. So don't ask only whether they've filed something: look at how much they've filed, and since when. Check it in a minute.

The social tells: they're easy to like

Fakes don't look fake. They look like the most connected person in the room. A room vouching for someone isn't a check. Paperwork is.

This applies to men and women alike. Look for the pattern, not the type:

  • Everywhere visible: parties, events, the VIP section, always in the right photos.
  • Exclusivity as the pitch: ‘by invitation,’ ‘closing soon,’ ‘I can get you in.’
  • Big claims, no names: 20 LPs, a huge fund, great returns, but no named co-investors, no portfolio companies you can verify.
  • Lifestyle as proof: the fancy car, the watches, the table. (Often rented or borrowed. They'll never say.)
  • Steady, too-good returns, or vague answers about how the money is made.
  • Name-dropping: past roles and famous friends that don't match public records.
  • Won't show documents: no offering memorandum, no fund agreement, no named lawyer or administrator.
  • Pressure to move fast, or to wire money to a personal account.
  • Asks you to bring friends.
  • Gets irritated or evasive when asked to be checked.

Words that sound like titles

Watch the euphemisms. They sound like job descriptions and mean ‘nothing yet’:

  • ‘Emerging’ VC or manager. Emerging managers are real, but they run a real first fund: a Form D, a first close, named LPs. ‘Emerging’ with none of those is just ‘aspiring.’
  • ‘In the process of raising’: ask when it started, how much has closed, and which filing shows it.
  • ‘Stealth’: nothing to check, by design. Startups go stealth; VC funds don’t. A real fund has a Form D and named LPs. A ‘stealth VC fund’ is red flag territory.
  • ‘Family office’ or ‘syndicate’ with no named members or filings. A ‘family office’ is the easiest cover of all: they’re rich, so the story goes, and they don’t like people asking for specifics, like what they actually do or who they did it for. Real family offices can be private, but someone asking you for money, introductions or work can say whose money it is and what it has backed.
  • ‘Advisor,’ ‘partner,’ ‘venture partner’: an advisory title is not a fund or a role in one.
  • ‘Exploring’ or ‘building’ a fund or a company: a plan, not an entity.

Charm is free. Filings aren't. Check the paperwork below.

Well connected and well respected means nothing

The victims of Bernie Madoff (150 years in prison) were not naive. They included Steven Spielberg's Wunderkinder Foundation, Elie Wiesel's foundation, Kevin Bacon and Kyra Sedgwick, Sandy Koufax, Larry King, Mets owner Fred Wilpon's family, John Malkovich, the charitable trust of New York Daily News owner Mortimer Zuckerman, major banks (Santander, HSBC, Royal Bank of Scotland, BNP Paribas), an insurer (AXA), universities (Yeshiva, NYU, Tufts, Bard) and charities. His own community, who trusted him most, was hit hard: Hadassah alone lost about $90 million. He was also registered with the SEC. Reputation, exclusivity and a filing did not protect any of them.*

Titles mean nothing

Fakes have grandiose titles and backgrounds: founder, partner, advisor, ‘family office.’ Ignore the title and look for the record. Anna Delvey (4 to 12 years in prison) passed as a wealthy heiress in New York for years, well connected and well liked, until someone checked the paperwork.

1. If they're raising money from anyone else, there's a Form D

A fund that takes money from outside investors (LPs) almost always files a Form D with the SEC within 15 days of its first sale. Unless a VC is investing only their own money, there should be one. A single outside LP is enough to need it. The Form D shows who the fund is, who signed, the people running it, the date of first sale, the amount raised and how many investors.

There is a way to raise money without filing a Form D (relying on the Section 4(a)(2) private placement exemption directly instead of the standard Reg D rules). It is risky for the fund and almost never done. And even then, a real fund usually shows up in other filings FURP finds anyway: Form ADV, SEC filings, 990s, registrations, and the people who run it.

No Form D is a red flag. No trace anywhere is the real tell.

2. If they advise funds, check Form ADV

Fund managers and advisers file Form ADV, either registered or as an exempt reporting adviser. Look them up.

3. If they're “raising money for other VCs,” they need a broker-dealer license

Anyone paid to find investors for a fund is generally acting as a broker-dealer and must be licensed. FURP checks broker-dealer records too.

4. What to look for on the result

  • Do the people listed match who you're dealing with?
  • Is the amount raised and the date consistent with their story?
  • Is the fund new and, more important, does the fund manager have a track record? FURP traces it by name: search the managers’ names to see the funds, filings and roles they actually appear in.
  • No fund, or a negligible one? Some fake VCs have no fund at all. Others have one on paper with almost nothing in it. So don’t stop at finding a filing: look at when the fund was established and how much money it actually has (the amount sold on the Form D, the assets on Form ADV).

LinkedIn is never a source. Fakes collect connections, and every connection brings them more. Joining the club takes one click on ‘Connect.’ A big network and a polished profile are a self-described background, nothing more.

Schools are a common trick. Many people take a short open-enrollment course or a one-time seminar at a well-known school, then list that school at the top of their LinkedIn, and others take them for an alum.

Never put much, if any, stock in LinkedIn. It is their own description of themselves.

Names don't matter much. Anna Delvey was a made-up name (she was born Anna Sorokin), and for years nobody cared. Check the record, not the name.

Vouching helps, up to a point. If people you know and trust vouch for someone, that may be OK. But Elizabeth Holmes (11 years and 3 months in prison) had glowing recommendations and a board that included Henry Kissinger, George Shultz and James Mattis. Stanford professor Phyllis Gardner told her early on that her idea wouldn’t work. Holmes cited her famous backers, never her critics.**

We’ve heard of ‘VCs’ writing $1,500 checks and then demanding introductions, board updates, reports and everything else a real lead investor would get. A filing that shows a tiny or brand-new fund is a warning, not a pass.

5. Then check everything else, not just the SEC

The SEC isn't the only place to look. FURP also shows the nonprofits, filings and people connected to a name, and searches patents, trademarks and business filings in several states. A real investor or company leaves traces in many of these; a fake leaves none, or a thin, recent one.

Then check how far back they go: look at their website and pages on the Wayback Machine (https://web.archive.org/). If a ‘ten-year track record’ first appears online last spring, that's the answer.

Paperwork is a start, not proof

Having a filing doesn't mean someone isn't a fake. Not having one isn't definitive either. Use extreme caution either way. But checking the filings is the best first step to protect yourself: it takes a minute, costs nothing, and most fakes fail it.
